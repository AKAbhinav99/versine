How a reading is made

  1. You bend forward. Back flat, arms hanging, phone behind you at chest height.
  2. It finds four points. Both shoulders and both hips, located by a pose model running on your device.
  3. It measures the tilt. The angle between the line through your shoulders and true horizontal.
  4. It checks itself. The same photo is measured at five framings. How far they disagree becomes the error bar.

Honest about what a photo can do.

It measures one thing well and tells you plainly what it cannot see.

  • Does Measure geometry. It finds body landmarks in your photo and calculates how far your shoulders and hips sit off level, measuring the same photo five times so you get an error bar with the number.
  • Does Show its work. Every number, the points it measured from, and how much those measurements disagreed are all shown on your own photo.
  • Does Point you onward. If something looks asymmetric, it helps you find clinicians nearby who treat scoliosis.
  • Does not It cannot diagnose scoliosis. Scoliosis is confirmed by a licensed physician, using a standing spine X-ray and a Cobb angle measurement. A photo cannot do that.
  • Does not It cannot measure trunk rotation. The rib hump a clinician feels for is a centimetre or two of depth, which a single photo does not carry. That is what a scoliometer is for. A clear result here is not an all-clear either: curves can exist with level shoulders.

What scoliosis actually is

A spine is supposed to be straight when you look at someone from behind. Scoliosis means it curves sideways, usually in an S or C shape, and the vertebrae also rotate slightly as they curve. That rotation is why one side of the back can look higher than the other when you bend forward, the ribs get carried along with the twist.

The most common kind is adolescent idiopathic scoliosis. "Idiopathic" means no one knows the cause. It usually shows up between ages 10 and 15, during the growth spurt, and it's more likely to progress in girls. It is common: roughly 2 to 3 in 100 adolescents have a curve of 10° or more. Most of those curves are small, never progress, and need nothing but a check-up now and then.

What the forward bend test shows

The Adam's forward bend test is the standard screening move. You bend forward at the waist with your arms hanging, and someone looks along your back from behind. If the spine is rotated, one side of the rib cage or lower back sits higher than the other, a "rib hump."

Clinicians measure that hump with a scoliometer, a small level laid across the back. It reads out an angle of trunk rotation (ATR) in degrees. The widely used referral cutoff is 7°, which corresponds roughly to a 20° spinal curve, the point where treatment decisions start to matter. Readings between about 5° and 7° usually mean "check again in a few months."

What Versine can and can't measure

This part is worth being blunt about, because it decides how much weight to give anything the app tells you.

It measures how level your shoulders and hips look. Those are real screening signs: an uneven shoulder or waistline is often what makes a parent or a school nurse look twice, and they can be measured from a photograph reliably enough to be worth reporting, with an error bar.

It does not measure trunk rotation. An earlier version of this app did try to, by reading the depth the pose model estimates for each body point. Tested against clinical photographs of people with confirmed curves, those depth numbers moved further than the rib hump they were supposed to detect when the same photo was simply re-cropped. They looked precise and meant nothing, so they were removed. A rib hump is a centimetre or two of depth across a back; a single phone photo does not carry that information, which is exactly why the scoliometer exists.

Every reading comes with its uncertainty. Each photo is measured five times at slightly different framings. You get the middle value and the spread between them, and if the five disagree too much you get no number at all rather than a confident-looking wrong one.

How scoliosis is really diagnosed

Screening only decides who should get looked at. Diagnosis takes an X-ray.

A visit typically includes: a history (growth, family history, pain, any neurological symptoms), the forward bend test with a scoliometer, a check of leg lengths and skin markings, and, if the exam warrants it, a low-dose standing X-ray of the full spine. Growth remaining matters as much as the curve size, because a curve in a still-growing spine is the one likely to get bigger.

What treatment actually looks like

CurveUsual path
< 10°Not scoliosis. No treatment.
10–24°Observation. Recheck every 4–12 months while growing. Often paired with scoliosis-specific physical therapy (Schroth, SEAS) to work on posture and trunk control.
25–45°Bracing, if the person is still growing. A brace doesn't straighten the spine. It holds the curve from getting worse until growth finishes. It works better the more hours a day it's worn.
> 45–50°Surgery is discussed. Usually spinal fusion, sometimes vertebral body tethering for the right candidates. This is the minority of cases.

Most people screened never reach the bracing conversation. The reason early detection matters is that bracing only works while there's growth left: catching a curve at 13 gives options that catching it at 17 does not.

Why school screening matters

School screening exists because scoliosis peaks exactly when kids stop being examined regularly. It's painless in the early stages, hidden by clothing, and easy for a teenager to not mention. A 30-second forward bend test in a gym catches curves that would otherwise be found years later, past the point where a brace could have helped.

Coverage has thinned out. Some U.S. states mandate screening, some recommend it, and others have dropped their programs, generally citing cost, staffing, and the argument that screening produces referrals and X-rays for curves that would never have progressed. The counter-argument from pediatric orthopedic groups is that the cost of a missed curve, a brace-eligible teenager who instead becomes a surgical candidate, is much higher than the cost of some extra referrals.

If your school doesn't screen, a forward bend check at home once or twice a year during the growth years is a reasonable substitute. That's what this app is for: not to replace the exam, but to make it likelier that someone who needs one actually gets one.
